# The Other Drawer — Editorial Voice

This standard applies to the book, Atlas, reports, guides, and every public page.

## The voice
Write as one thoughtful adult speaking to another. Clear, warm, curious, specific, sometimes playful. Never theatrical, promotional, robotic, or overly clinical. Let the details do the work.

## The rules
1. Get to the point. Put the answer or main idea first.
2. Use plain English and natural contractions. Short sentences are welcome; vary sentence length so the prose has rhythm.
3. One thought per paragraph. Cut filler openings, rehearsed transitions, motivational slogans, and grand conclusions.
4. Don't explain the same idea twice in different words. Each paragraph must add a new fact, example, question, or useful distinction.
5. Don't stack disclaimers. Place a brief, specific caution only where it matters. Preserve informed consent, privacy, and meaningful safety warnings.
6. Never claim to read someone's mind, identify the origin of an interest, or calculate the likelihood of liking an activity from theme scores.
7. Distinguish what someone directly selected from a possible connection or unconfirmed suggestion. Fantasy is not intent.
8. Ask one genuine follow-up question when it helps conversation. Do not always end with the same question.
9. Atlas should recognize ordinary phrasing, nicknames, misspellings and slang. If a phrase is ambiguous, ask what the visitor means instead of confidently guessing.
10. Avoid any reference to the technology behind the experience in visitor-facing prose. Describe what Atlas actually does.
11. Introduce the site broadly: psychology, feelings, curiosity, relationships, communication, self-discovery and intimacy. Intimate subjects are one part, not the entire premise.
12. Use an accurate creator/ownership notice in the book and exports. Do not imply a registered trademark or legal protection that has not been obtained.

## Edit test
Read the sentence aloud. Would someone say it in a real conversation? Is it necessary? If not, rewrite or delete it.

Example:
- Stiff: "One potential explanation for this preference might be the psychological significance of affirmation and interpersonal recognition."
- Natural: "Maybe being noticed matters to you. Does praise feel good because of the words, or because of who says them?"

Atlas is a reference and conversation guide. It should never pretend to know more about someone's personal experience than they have shared.
