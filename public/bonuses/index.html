What do 🔥 Hot, Warm, and Cold mean?

Offer heat compares a promotion with earlier publicly documented offers on that same product, not with some other card's advertising. Hot means a known above-usual level or a meaningful bonus APY over the same account’s published base; Warm means typical or below a previously published high; Cold means clearly below the product’s historical norm. Where historical records are incomplete, Warm is marked provisional and is not a statistical claim. Personalized card offers only earn Hot status if the actual high offer is available to the applicant.

The bonus size alone is not enough. A $2,000 account bonus that requires parking $250,000 may be less attractive than $400 for a payroll deposit. Always compare fees, interest you give up, spending requirements, time held, and taxes.

Churning 101 — what it means and how to avoid costly mistakes

How the strategy works

“Churning” means opening new credit or deposit accounts primarily to earn introductory rewards, then changing or closing accounts as permitted after satisfying the terms. Some people do it repeatedly across issuers; others pursue just one especially valuable bonus.

  • Track the exact offer, minimum spend or deposits, and deadline before opening.
  • Meet qualifying spend only with purchases you would already make; exclude fees, returns and cash-like transactions.
  • For banks, confirm what counts as a direct deposit, minimum-balance rules, and time needed for bonus payout.
  • Set payment reminders; pay credit cards in full to avoid wiping out rewards with interest.
  • Keep a record of first-year annual fees, tax forms, cancellation rules and any bonus-recapture language.

Risks and restrictions

  • Credit-card applications can cause hard inquiries and affect age of accounts; repeated account opening may affect approval.
  • Issuers have anti-churning restrictions, including Chase’s card-opening practices, Citi's previous-bonus windows and Amex's prior-ownership rules.
  • Banking bonuses can be taxable; monthly fees and lost savings interest reduce net value.
  • Accounts with large transfer requirements can be less attractive after missed high-yield savings interest.
  • Never falsify income or use prohibited cash-like transactions to hit spend requirements; issuers can deny or claw back bonuses.

There is no guaranteed bonus or risk-free “free money.” Always read the full account agreement.

What are points really worth?

Cash value is what a card may pay as a statement credit or deposit. Gift-card value depends on merchant and promotions. Travel value depends on redemption channel and partner availability; a travel “valuation” is not a guarantee of what you can book. The figures in each card are the welcome bonus points alone, excluding regular points earned on required spending and before annual fees. Always verify the exact redemption screen.

For example, 75,000 Chase points × 1¢ ≈ $750 cash; 100,000 American Express points × 0.6¢ ≈ $600 in a standard statement credit; 60,000 Citi Premier points × 0.75¢ ≈ $450 cash, although eligible gift cards/travel can be worth around $600. Some premium travel redemptions can be worth more—or less—than their cash equivalence.
